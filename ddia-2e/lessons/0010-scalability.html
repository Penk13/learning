DDIA · Ch.2 Defining Nonfunctional Requirements

Scalability

"Is it scalable?" is the wrong question. Describe the load, then ask what your options are as it grows.

Never say "X scales" or "Y doesn't scale". Ask: "If the system grows in this particular way, what are our options, and when do we hit the limits?"

For a small new product, keep it simple and flexible. Worrying about hypothetical scale is premature optimization and can lock you into an inflexible design. Learn where the real bottlenecks are first.

Step 1 · Describe the load

Usually throughput (requests/s, GB/day, checkouts/hour), sometimes a peak (simultaneous online users). Plus statistics that shape access patterns: read:write ratio, cache hit rate, items per user (followers!). Sometimes averages matter; sometimes a few extreme cases dominate. Then ask:

  1. Load grows and resources stay fixed: what happens to performance?
  2. Load grows: how many more resources keep performance the same?

Doubling resources to handle double the load is linear scalability (good). Usually cost grows faster than linearly.

Step 3 · Principles

  • No "magic scaling sauce". 100k requests/s of 1 kB each looks nothing like 3 requests/min of 2 GB each, despite the same 100 MB/s.
  • Expect to rethink the architecture at every order-of-magnitude increase in load; don't plan more than ~10× ahead.
  • Break the system into parts that operate largely independently: the idea behind microservices, sharding, stream processing and shared-nothing. The hard part is where to draw the lines.
  • Don't over-complicate. A single-machine database that does the job beats a distributed setup. Autoscaling is neat, but predictable load may be calmer with manual scaling. 5 services are simpler than 50.

Several machines, each with its own CPU and RAM, share one disk array over a SAN. Which architecture is this?

Independent compute with common storage over a fast network (NAS/SAN) is shared-disk. Locking and contention limit how far it scales.

Why does DDIA call "Is this system scalable?" the wrong question, and what should you ask instead?

Scalability isn't a yes/no label. Ask: if the load grows in this particular way, what options do we have to cope, and when do we hit the limits? That requires first describing the load with concrete numbers.

Once you've described a system's load, which two questions does DDIA say to ask as it grows? What is linear scalability?

(1) If load grows and resources stay the same, how does performance change? (2) If load grows, how much must resources grow to keep performance unchanged? Linear scalability: doubling resources handles double the load at the same performance.

Give the main advantages and the main costs of a shared-nothing (scale-out) architecture.

Advantages: can scale linearly, uses the best price/performance hardware, easy to grow or shrink, fault tolerance across machines or datacenters. Costs: data must be explicitly sharded, and you take on all the complexity of distributed systems.
