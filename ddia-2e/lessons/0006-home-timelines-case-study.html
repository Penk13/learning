DDIA · Ch.2 Defining Nonfunctional Requirements

Case Study: Social Network Home Timelines

Nonfunctional requirements made concrete: do the work when reading, or when writing?

What vs how well

Functional requirements say what the app does: screens, buttons, operations. Nonfunctional requirements say how well: fast, reliable, secure, compliant, easy to maintain. They're often unwritten because they seem obvious, but an app that's unbearably slow or unreliable might as well not exist. The chapter covers four: performance, reliability, scalability and maintainability (security is mostly outside the book).

Functional: "students can register for a class." Nonfunctional: "…in under a second at p99, even on registration day, even if a server dies, and next year's team can still change it."

The case: an X/Twitter-style network

Users post and follow each other. Assumptions: 500M posts/day ≈ 5,800 posts/s (spikes up to 150k/s); the average user follows 200 people and has 200 followers, but a few celebrities have 100M+. Followers should see a post within 5 seconds.

When one request triggers many downstream requests, the multiplication factor is the fan-out. Precomputing a query's result and keeping it updated is materialization; the timeline cache is a materialized view.

🌟 Celebrities

Fanning out to 100M timelines is too much. Store celebrity posts separately and merge them in at read time: a hybrid.

📚 Super-followers

Someone following thousands of busy accounts won't read everything, so it's OK to drop some writes and show a sample.

⚡ Spikes

Enqueue timeline deliveries. Posts show up a bit later, but reads stay fast because they come from the cache.

Do work at write time when that makes the frequent operation cheap. But the extreme cases, not the average, often decide the design.

In DDIA's social-network case study, why materialize home timelines instead of polling a join query?

5.8k posts/s × 200 followers ≈ 1.16M timeline writes/s, versus 2M polls/s × 200 follows = 400M lookups/s. Pay once at write time; reads become a cache hit.

Define fan-out and materialized view, using a social-network home timeline as the example.

Fan-out: the factor by which one request multiplies into downstream requests; one new post becomes one write per follower. Materialized view: a precomputed query result kept up to date; each user's timeline cache, updated whenever someone they follow posts.

With fan-out on write, how does the design handle celebrities with 100M followers, and sudden posting spikes?

Celebrity posts aren't fanned out: they're stored separately and merged into the timeline at read time. Spikes are absorbed by queueing the timeline deliveries, so posts appear a little later while reads stay fast from the cache.
