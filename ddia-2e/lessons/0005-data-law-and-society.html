DDIA · Ch.1 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture

Data Systems, Law and Society

Architecture is shaped not only by business needs but by the rights of the people whose data you hold.

Laws such as the GDPR (EU, 2018), the CCPA and the EU AI Act set rules for personal data, and automated decisions about loans, jobs or policing affect people's lives. These rules reach deep into system design.

🗑 Right to be forgotten

GDPR lets people demand erasure of their data. But many systems are built on immutable, append-only logs. How do you delete from the middle of an immutable file, or from derived data such as ML training sets? There's no settled answer yet.

💸 True cost of storing data

Not just the storage bill: liability if it leaks, reputational damage, fines for non-compliance, government demands for access, and real safety risks for users (e.g. location history).

✂ Data minimization

Datensparsamkeit: some data isn't worth keeping, so delete it. The opposite of "store everything just in case". It fits the GDPR: collect only for a specified purpose, don't reuse it for other purposes, don't keep it longer than needed.

Industry standards push the same way: PCI for card payments, SOC 2 Type 2 audits for software vendors. The book returns to ethics in its final chapter.

Local note (not from the book). Indonesia has its own Personal Data Protection Law (UU PDP, No. 27/2022). Check specifics with the relevant BINUS compliance or legal function rather than assuming.

Before storing personal data, ask: why do we keep it, for how long, and can we actually delete it everywhere, including every derived copy?

Why is the GDPR "right to be forgotten" technically hard for many data systems?

Many systems are built on immutable, append-only logs, which can't simply be edited in the middle. And the data is copied into derived systems (caches, indexes, warehouses, ML training sets), so it must be found and removed everywhere. There is no settled solution.

What is data minimization (Datensparsamkeit), and which GDPR principles does it match?

Keeping only the data you actually need and deleting the rest, instead of "store everything just in case". It matches the GDPR's purpose limitation (collect for a specified purpose, don't reuse for others) and storage limitation (don't keep it longer than necessary).

A team wants to log every user's location history indefinitely "because storage is cheap".

Which costs of storing that data are they ignoring?

Liability and reputational damage if it leaks, fines for non-compliance with data protection law, exposure to government demands for the data, and real safety risks to users whose location history could be misused.
