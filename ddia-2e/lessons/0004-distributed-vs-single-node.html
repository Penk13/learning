DDIA · Ch.1 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture

Distributed vs Single-Node Systems

Nine good reasons to spread a system over many machines, and the heavy price you pay for doing it.

A distributed system is several machines communicating over a network; each participating process is a node.

Nine reasons to distribute

ReasonWhat it means
InherentMany users on their own devices
Between servicesData stored in one service, processed in another
Fault toleranceOne machine dies, another takes over
ScalabilityMore data or compute than one machine can handle
LatencyServers near users worldwide
ElasticityScale up and down with demand; pay for what you use
Special hardwareDisk-heavy storage nodes, CPU-heavy analytics, GPUs for ML
LegalData residency laws: keep citizens' data in-country
SustainabilityRun jobs where and when renewable power is plentiful
The price of distribution
  • Any network call can fail or time out, and then you don't know whether the request was processed. Blindly retrying may be unsafe.
  • A network call is vastly slower than a function call. A single-threaded program on one machine can beat a 100+ core cluster.
  • Debugging is hard, so you need observability (tracing with OpenTelemetry, Zipkin, Jaeger).
  • When each service has its own database, consistency across services becomes the app's problem.

Don't rush to distribute. Hardware has grown huge and fast; with single-node databases like SQLite or DuckDB, many workloads fit on one machine.

Microservices and serverless

🧩 Microservices

Each service: one purpose, an API, one owning team, its own database (a shared database makes its schema part of everyone's API).

👍 Independent deploys, right-sized resources, freedom to change internals. 👎 Hard to test, per-service infrastructure (deploys, logs, monitoring, on-call), APIs hard to evolve.

A technical solution to a people problem: it lets many teams move independently. A small organization with few teams probably doesn't need it.

⚡ Serverless (FaaS)

The cloud allocates resources per request and you pay only while your code runs: metered billing for compute.

👎 Execution time limits, restricted runtimes, slow cold starts. It still runs on servers, just not the same one each time.

Cloud vs supercomputing (HPC). HPC runs huge batch jobs (weather, molecular simulation), checkpoints them, and on a node failure stops everything and restarts from the checkpoint. It trusts its users. Cloud services must stay continuously available, isolate tenants who don't trust each other, and may span regions. The book is about the cloud kind.

According to DDIA, microservices are primarily…

They let many teams progress independently. With few teams they're often unnecessary overhead.

Your service calls another service and the request times out. What do you know about whether the work was done, and why does that matter?

Nothing: the request may have been lost, processed with the reply lost, or still be running. So a blind retry may do the work twice and can be unsafe.

Give five of the nine reasons DDIA lists for building a distributed system.

Inherent distribution (users on many devices), work between services, fault tolerance, scalability, low latency for users worldwide, elasticity, special hardware, legal data residency, and sustainability (run where renewable power is plentiful).

A three-person team proposes splitting a modest internal app into twelve microservices, each with its own database, "so it scales".

What would DDIA push back with?

Microservices mainly solve a people problem (many teams moving independently), which a three-person team doesn't have. They add costs: network failures and timeouts, slower calls, harder testing and debugging, per-service operations, and cross-service consistency handled by the app. If the workload fits one node, a single-node design is simpler and may be faster.
